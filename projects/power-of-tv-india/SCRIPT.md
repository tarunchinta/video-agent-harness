# "The Soap Opera Effect" — Script Draft

Short-form explainer (~90 seconds, ~240 spoken words at 160 wpm).
Topic: Jensen & Oster, "The Power of TV: Cable Television and Women's Status in India" (QJE 2009; NBER w13305, 2007).
Stake: BROAD. Anyone who watches TV has a stake in whether it quietly changes what they think is normal.
Spine: follow one woman (an archetype, see Notes) from "needs permission" to the results, then the honest caveats.

---

## BEAT 1 — HOOK (0:00–0:08)

**VO:** It's 2001. A woman in a village in rural India needs permission to go to the market. Then cable TV shows up.

**Visual:** Dusty village lane, one woman at a doorway. A cable line gets strung across the street above her. Caption: "Rural India, 2001".

---

## BEAT 2 — TURN (0:08–0:22)

**VO:** Two years later, women in villages like hers say they need permission less often. Fewer say it's OK for a husband to beat his wife. Fewer say they want their next child to be a son.

**Visual:** Three survey cards flip in sequence, each tick moving the "after cable" direction: permission, wife-beating, son preference.

---

## BEAT 3 — PROOF / HOW THEY KNOW (0:22–0:37)

**VO:** Economists Robert Jensen and Emily Oster followed about 2,700 households in 180 villages from 2001 to 2003. When it started, 65 villages had cable. Some got it during the survey and some never did, so they compared the two groups.

**Visual:** Grid of 180 dots. 65 lit at the start; more light up through 2002 and 2003; the rest stay dark. Split screen: "got cable" vs. "still waiting".

---

## BEAT 4 — REHOOK (0:37–0:49)

**VO:** But anyone can tell a surveyor what sounds right. So they also checked what people did. More girls were enrolled in school. Women had fewer babies, mostly because they waited longer between them.

**Visual:** Text bubble "the right answer" dissolves. Cut to a school enrollment bar ticking up, then a family timeline where the gap between births stretches.

---

## BEAT 5 — WHY TV (0:49–1:10)

**VO:** For some answers, the shift was about as big as five extra years of school. And nobody on TV told anyone to have fewer kids. The hit serials, like Kyunki Saas Bhi Kabhi Bahu Thi, were set in cities, where women worked, ran businesses and handled money. The authors think viewers copied a life that looked good.

**Visual:** "≈ 5 years of school" stack of textbooks. Then a TV in the village home playing a stylised (not real-footage) city drama: an office, a ledger, a woman at a desk. Lower third: show title, Star Plus, 2000.

---

## BEAT 6 — CAVEATS (1:10–1:25)

**VO:** Two catches. Cable wasn't handed out at random, so maybe those villages were changing anyway. The authors checked the earlier trends and say no. And a 2014 replication found no effect at all for women with no schooling.

**Visual:** Two warning cards: "Not randomized" with a pre-trend line chart, and "Replication: Iversen & Palmer-Jones, 2014" with a small chart split by education.

---

## BEAT 7 — CLOSER (1:25–1:30)

**VO:** Nobody told her to want a different life. She just got to watch one.

**Visual:** Back to the woman at the doorway; the TV glow on her face. She steps out toward the market. End card: paper title and year.

---

## Notes

- The woman is an archetype standing in for the survey's respondents, not a real person.
- The paper describes popular serials in general. It isn't confirmed that it names Kyunki; the script uses it as an example of the era's biggest urban-set serial ("like"), not as the paper's own example. See RESEARCH.md.
- "About five years of school" is the abstract's cross-sectional comparison for some outcomes, so the VO limits it to "some answers".
- The caveat beat is load-bearing. Cutting it would overstate a natural experiment as proof.
